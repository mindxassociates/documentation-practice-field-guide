01 · The foundation

Documentation has several jobs.

Records support assessment, planning and service delivery, accountability, coordination, supervision, and evaluation. A well-kept record helps another qualified professional understand what happened and why.

Write for the record’s real audience

Clients, future providers, practice partners, insurers, utilization reviewers, and legal decision-makers may have legitimate access. Keep the note relevant, clear, and proportionate. Avoid gratuitous details that do not serve care or accountability.

Ask: Would a reader understand the clinical need, what I did, the client’s response, and the next step?

Use the FACT check

A simple quality screen for every entry:

FFactual
AAccurate
CComplete
TTimely

Separate observation from interpretation

Attribute reports to the client; describe observable behavior concretely; label your clinical assessment as an assessment. Replace broad labels such as “aggressive,” “noncompliant,” or “doing well” with specific behaviors, context, and evidence.

Precision protects meaning

Ambiguous grammar, unexplained abbreviations, and vague pronouns can change the meaning of a note. Use plain language, identify who did what, and proofread names, dates, pronouns, and negations.

03 · Systems, privacy & risk

The note lives inside a larger system.

Sound documentation depends on consistent office procedures, secure technology, clear client expectations, and a plan for unusual events.

Keep a complete clinical record

  • Intake, history, assessment, informed consent, treatment plan, progress, and changes in goals.
  • Client and third-party contacts, consultation, services provided, decisions, and referrals.
  • Critical incidents, missed or canceled visits, and clinically significant changes.
  • Relevant evaluations, fee and payment information, termination, final assessment, and supporting documents.

Set clear practice policies

  • Define documentation standards, timelines, correction procedures, record retention, storage, access, and release.
  • Explain confidentiality and its limits, third-party access, portal use, and social media boundaries.
  • Establish procedures for subpoenas, requests for records, and consultation with counsel.
  • Review policies against your jurisdiction’s laws, ethics code, payer contracts, and practice model.

Electronic records & client portals

  • Understand templates, auto-populated fields, alerts, reminders, metadata, and how information is retrieved.
  • Avoid copy-paste drift; verify dates and imported details; document why an alert was overridden when clinically relevant.
  • Use access controls, automatic lockout, secure devices, workforce training, and appropriate audit logs.
  • Set portal response expectations, define uploads, and state clearly that portals are not monitored for emergencies.

Telehealth & electronic communication

  • Document appropriateness, risks and benefits, client location, identity, privacy, and a backup plan for technology failure.
  • Discuss secure networks and private spaces, limits of nonvisual communication, and risks of interception or forwarding.
  • Clarify emergency contacts, what to do if connection drops, and when remote care is not adequate.
  • Explain confidentiality limits and any recording policy. Reassess fit over time.

AI tools, searches & digital traces

  • Before using AI or third-party software, assess confidentiality, consent, data retention, security, accuracy, bias, and professional accountability.
  • Never assume generated content is accurate. Review, correct, and take responsibility for every entry signed under your name.
  • Have a clear clinical rationale and policy for online searches; document relevant actions and decisions proportionately.
  • Remember that messages, metadata, edits, and access logs may be discoverable or retained.

Higher-risk situations

  • When safety concerns, critical incidents, service interruptions, or legal action arise, document promptly and carefully. False claims or altered records can bring serious legal, financial, and licensing consequences.
  • Record the information considered, assessment, consultation, actions, rationale, referrals, and follow-up plan.
  • For couples and families, establish the recordkeeping approach in advance; consider a group summary and separate individual entries when an event affects one member.
  • Document boundaries related to gifts, invitations, social media, and other significant interactions.

Common legal and regulatory touchpoints

Examples that may affect access, privacy, retention, and disclosure include HIPAA, the 21st Century Cures Act, 42 CFR Part 2, FERPA, state mental health confidentiality laws, privilege rules, and court procedures for electronically stored information. Which rules apply depends on your setting, role, jurisdiction, and the facts.

04 · Put it into practice

Choose a note structure that fits.

Formats are prompts, not substitutes for clinical judgment. Follow payer, jurisdictional, and practice requirements.

SOAP

Subjective report · Objective information · Assessment · Plan

DAP

Data · Assessment · Plan

Progress note

New needs · current assessment · interventions · response and progress · plan or goal changes
